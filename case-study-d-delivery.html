Case study · Logistics

Two videos, and the argument is over.

Every delivery dispute is the same dispute: it arrived broken, or it never arrived. D. records the parcel at pickup and again at drop-off, and those two videos are what an insurance claim is settled with.

In short

D. is a Kotlin Multiplatform delivery and logistics platform built as three pieces: a customer app for booking, live tracking and payment; a rider app for accepting jobs, navigating and recording proof; and a web control tower for dispatch, the ledger and disputes.

Its differentiator is structural rather than cosmetic. Every delivery requires two mandatory video recordings — one at pickup, one at drop-off — stored server side. Those videos are the backbone of a parcel insurance product, which is only possible because the evidence is captured by default instead of requested after something goes wrong.

It carries real money through cash on delivery, real legal identifiers such as device IMEI, and real physical goods, so correctness is enforced at the database rather than assumed in the app.

Three apps, one source of truth.

Why courier disputes never resolve.

A customer says the laptop arrived with a cracked screen. The rider says it was handed over intact. The company has a delivery timestamp and a signature scrawled on a phone, neither of which says anything about the condition of the item.

So the company does one of two things. It refuses the claim, and loses the customer. Or it pays out on every claim, and becomes a target. Both are expensive, and both happen because the evidence was never captured at the moment it existed.

Insurance is impossible on top of that. You cannot underwrite a parcel when nobody can establish what condition it was in when it changed hands.

Decisions that shaped it.

Video proof is mandatory, not optional

A rider cannot complete a job without recording at pickup and at drop-off. Making it optional would mean it is captured on the deliveries that go well and missing on exactly the ones that matter. The videos go to server-side storage rather than the rider's camera roll, so the evidence does not belong to the person it might be used against.

Money is never a raw floating-point number

Financial values are rounded deliberately at defined boundaries rather than accumulating floating-point error across a ledger. On a cash-on-delivery platform, a fraction of a rupee per order becomes a reconciliation problem at volume, and it is the kind of bug that is nearly impossible to unwind once a month of orders has been settled on top of it.

The database is the source of truth, not the Kotlin model

Every domain model and UI state is treated as a projection of Postgres state. When Kotlin and the database disagree, the database is right. That sounds obvious and is routinely violated: an app that trusts its own cached model is an app that shows a rider a job that was reassigned four minutes ago.

Riders work offline, because delivery happens in dead zones

The rider app is built offline-first with a local store and a sync queue. Stairwells, basements and industrial estates are where parcels actually change hands, and an app that needs a connection to record a handover fails precisely where it is needed.

Dispatch expands outward instead of broadcasting to everyone

A new order is offered to the closest available riders first and the radius widens if nobody accepts. Broadcasting to the whole fleet at once looks simpler and produces a race that annoys every rider who lost it.

What it changes commercially.

  • Insurance becomes a product, not a liability. Because condition is evidenced at both ends of every journey by default, a parcel insurance offering can be priced instead of guessed at.
  • Disputes stop being someone's word. A claim is resolved by watching two short videos, which takes a support agent a minute rather than a week of correspondence.
  • Riders are paid on arithmetic. The wallet and settlement ledger compute what is owed from recorded jobs rather than from an end-of-week reckoning.
  • Pricing is defensible. Distance comes from a routing API, not a straight line, with explicit surcharges for weight and fragility. A customer who asks why a delivery cost what it did gets an answer.
  • High-value goods become carryable. Recording device identifiers such as IMEI alongside video evidence is what makes moving electronics a business rather than a risk.
