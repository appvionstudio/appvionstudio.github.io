Case study · Enterprise operations

Where the paywall is a database trigger, not a screen.

SnowGrid runs a snow-removal business as a live network: property owners, dispatchers, contractors and drivers on one grid. The hard part was never the map. It was making sure no one can lie to the server.

In short

SnowGrid is a multi-tenant B2B platform that lets a snow-removal company run its entire operation from one Android app. Four roles share it: super admin, dispatcher, contractor and customer. Property owners trace their own lot on a map, the app returns area and perimeter, and a pricing engine turns snow depth plus surface area plus priority into a quotable figure the customer authorises on the spot.

It is production-grade with 46 database migrations applied, and the commercial rules live in Postgres rather than in Kotlin: job prices are recomputed server-side, money splits are calculated by a database trigger, and plan limits are BEFORE INSERT triggers. A company admin cannot award itself a paid plan even though it holds write access to its own row.

The problem with snow.

Snow removal is an industry that runs on phone calls at four in the morning. A storm lands, a dispatcher starts ringing round contractors, property owners ring the dispatcher to ask whether anyone is coming, and the price for each job is negotiated per call by whoever picks up.

Three things break at once. Nobody agrees what a job costs, because the price was agreed verbally under time pressure. Nobody can prove the work happened, because the evidence is a contractor's word. And nobody knows what they are owed, because the money split was worked out on paper after the season ended.

Software that only shows a map on a screen does not fix any of that. It has to be the thing that decides the price, holds the evidence, and computes the split.

Four decisions that made it trustworthy.

Anyone can build screens. These are the choices that decide whether a platform survives contact with people who have a financial reason to game it.

The price is computed on the server, and the client's number is thrown away

When a customer submits a job, the app sends the parameters, not the total. A database insert trigger recomputes the price from the pricing matrix and discards whatever the client claimed the total was. It is one of the oldest holes in commerce software — trusting a figure that travelled through a device the customer controls — and closing it is a three-line decision that has to be made before the first payment, not after the first dispute.

Money splits are a database trigger, and the shares sum exactly

Contractor share, company share and platform share are computed by a SECURITY DEFINER trigger, never sent by the client. They are also derived so that the parts sum to the job total exactly, rather than each being rounded independently — the classic way a ledger drifts by a cent per job until nobody can reconcile a season.

The paywall lives in Postgres, so the app cannot be tricked into granting a plan

A company admin legitimately holds update rights on its own company row. That means any plan check written in Kotlin can be bypassed by anyone willing to talk to the API directly. So plan_tier and plan_expires_at sit inside a trigger that rejects self-promotion, and the free-tier ceilings — five contractors, thirty customers — are BEFORE INSERT triggers that raise a real error. They are deliberately not implemented by hiding rows through row-level security, because that fails silently and users never learn why the app stopped working.

Cross-tenant access needs a second factor, enforced in the database

The super admin can read across every tenant, which makes that one account the most valuable credential in the system. Database policy requires an aal2 session — a verified TOTP factor — before any cross-tenant read resolves. A stolen password on its own grants nothing. Local storage is encrypted with SQLCipher, auth tokens sit in a three-tier Keystore strategy, and a rooted device is blocked at launch.

What it changes for the business running it.

  • Pricing stops being a negotiation. Depth, area and priority produce a number the customer authorises before anyone drives anywhere. The argument about what was agreed at 4am cannot happen, because nothing was agreed verbally.
  • Proof replaces trust. Before and after photographs are attached to the job by the contractor on site. A disputed job is settled by looking at it.
  • The season reconciles itself. Because every split was computed by the same trigger and the parts always sum to the total, the ledger at the end of a season is arithmetic rather than an investigation.
  • Owners onboard themselves. A customer draws their own lot on the map and the app returns the measurement. That removes a site visit from the sales process entirely.
  • Growth is not gated by features. The free tier is limited by size — five contractors, thirty customers — and every capability is switched on. A small operator experiences the whole product, which is the only version of a free tier that has anything to sell.

Engineering targets held throughout: cold start under two seconds, screen transitions under 300ms, a loading state on screen within 100ms of any request, and crash and ANR rates inside Google's Play vitals thresholds.

What it is built with.

Clean Architecture with MVVM across five modules. The domain layer holds no Android dependency at all, ViewModels expose only immutable state, and every network call returns a typed result rather than throwing.
